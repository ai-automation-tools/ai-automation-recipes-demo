# Chapter 1: False Wake

The clip opened on a dishwasher halfway through its rinse, water slapping the racks in uneven handfuls. Under it, a television turned low enough to keep somebody company. A man said, "Hurts, though," and then the four seconds were up.

Nell tagged it APPLIANCE and SPEECH-NONWAKE and hit the space bar. The queue served the next one before her thumb came off the key.

Hurts, though. She could hear how the speaker had gotten there. Hearth was a forgiving word, all breath at the front and nothing much at the back, and the little cylinders woke up for anything shaped close enough: heart, hurt, Herb, her thing, hearse. Every time one woke without being asked, it sent four seconds of the room home to Halden Domestic, two before the false wake and two after, and Halden sent the four seconds here, to the third floor of what used to be the JCPenney at Northfield Commons, where sixty people in headphones sat in rows and decided what each one had been.

The counter in the corner of her screen said 1,108. The clock under it said 2:41. The floor wanted fourteen hundred by six.

She worked with the left earcup pushed back off her ear. The left ear was mostly ornamental now, a souvenir of eleven years running the board at WDAO before the station handed her job to a scheduling program and a voice that never needed the bathroom. It heard a thin whine of its own and not much else. The right ear did the work. The right ear could tell a microwave door from a car door by the latch, a toddler from a cat by the second syllable, rain on vinyl siding from rain on aluminum. She had been proud of it once, in a way she didn't mention at parties, back when she went to parties.

A dog's tags clinking on a steel bowl. PET. A woman counting pills under her breath, ten, eleven, twelve. SPEECH-NONWAKE. Then room tone and nothing, a refrigerator thinking to itself in somebody's kitchen in Tulsa or Fresno or Bangor, and she typed NSD and went on.

No speech detected. She typed it three or four hundred times a shift. That was the part nobody guessed when she told them about the job, back when she still told people about the job. They expected secrets. They expected fights and sex and confessions, and those came, but mostly what came was houses with no one talking in them. Kettles. Furnaces. A television left on in an empty room for the dog.

Her rule was not to picture the houses. She broke it maybe twice a night.

She had broken it at 12:15, on a clip of a woman saying "Please, not in front of," in a voice pressed flat and quiet, and then a sound Nell had played four times without deciding what it was. She had tagged it DISTRESS, which sent it up to a team in Austin, and the clip had left her queue the way they all did, without a reply. Nobody on the floor ever heard what happened to a DISTRESS. You flagged it and you got the next one. She'd been carrying the woman around for three hours anyway, turning over the last word. In front of who.

On her left, Mari Duarte split a sunflower seed with her front teeth and spat the shell into a paper cup she kept wedged against her monitor.

"Get a singing one yet?" Mari said, too loud, the way everyone on the floor talked, one ear in and one ear out.

"Not tonight."

"I got a guy singing to his fish. A whole song. Like a ballad." She cracked another seed. "I tagged it PET."

"Was the fish singing back?"

"Rude," Mari said, delighted, and turned back to her screen.

At three o'clock the headset chimed and Paul came on the floor channel, the way he did every hour, his voice arriving in sixty right ears at once. "Third floor. My people. Halfway there and change." Paul ran the night shift from Austin. Nobody on the floor had met him; he was a voice with a smile in it, the smile of a man in a quarter-zip who believed in hydration. "Little reminder on DISTRESS, we're seeing some over-flagging. If you can't tell, it's probably TV. Drink some water. You're doing great."

Mari mouthed *you're doing great* at her monitor with her eyes crossed. Nell drank some water.

* * *

The clip came up at 3:09.

It opened on a refrigerator compressor kicking on. Two notes: a low hum, then a second one a little sharp of it, as if the motor had asked a question and not liked the answer. Nell's hand had already moved toward APPLIANCE when the clock came in underneath. A cheap battery clock, its second hand ticking, and at one point in the circle the tick caught on itself and doubled. Tick. Tick. Tick-tick.

She took her hand off the keyboard.

A chair leg, dragged a few inches over linoleum, and stopped. Then a voice, low and close to the microphone, the way you talk to someone who is half asleep:

"Nellie?"

Four seconds. The waveform went flat and sat there, waiting for a label.

She played it again. The fridge asked its question. The clock caught and doubled. The chair. "Nellie?" She played it again with her eyes shut. She pulled the left earcup down over her bad ear, which was stupid, which added nothing but the whine, and played it a fourth time. She counted the plays on the edge of the desk with one finger, the way she used to count takes. At eleven she stopped, because the twelfth would be the one where she let herself say who it was.

The metadata pane sat on the right side of the screen, where nobody looked. Device class: Hearth Mini, gen 3. Region: 45404. Timestamp: 03:02:51 EDT. Seven minutes ago. A household ID, a firmware string. And in the flags column, where she was used to seeing nothing, or NEWDEV, or LOWBATT, a flag she had never seen before. CMP-P.

45404 was Old North Dayton. It was a lot of houses. It was a lot of refrigerators.

Her refrigerator was a Frigidaire from about 1998 that her landlord had promised to replace in two different years. It made that sound. It made exactly that sound, the second note sharp, because she had stood in front of it at two in the morning more nights than she wanted to count, holding the door open for the light. The clock was a starburst thing from the Goodwill on Salem Avenue. Five dollars. It caught at the forty. She had meant to fix it and then decided she liked it, that it was the one thing in the apartment that sounded like it was trying.

The kitchen was locked. She lived alone. Teddy downstairs had a key for emergencies, but Teddy was seventy-one and in bed by nine, and Teddy called her Miss Nell, and Teddy did not have that voice.

Danny had called her Nellie. Only Danny, since she was six and he was one and couldn't get his mouth around the L, and then forever after because it annoyed her. Danny had given her the Hearth two Christmases ago. He'd been doing installs for Halden then, driving a white van around Montgomery County with a stepladder and a tablet, and he'd stood on her counter in his socks to mount the thing above the cabinets where, he said, it would hear the whole room. She'd told him she didn't want a robot listening to her make toast. He'd said it was too late, everything was listening anyway, so you might as well get a timer out of it.

That was more than a year before the river. Before February.

There was a voicemail on her phone from the night of the river. One minute and fourteen seconds. She knew the length because the phone showed it to her every time she scrolled past, and she scrolled past it most days, and she had not pressed play. It would keep. That was the whole point of a voicemail. It kept.

It could be a podcast, somebody's true crime show playing on a phone left on her counter. She had not left a phone on her counter. It could be a television. There was no television in her kitchen. It could be a different fridge and a different clock, in a different kitchen in 45404, where a different man was waking up a different Nellie, and she would label it SPEECH-NONWAKE and never think about it again.

"You okay?" Mari had a seed between her teeth and wasn't cracking it. "You've been on one clip forever."

"There's an echo. Can't tell if it's TV."

"Tag it TV. Nobody's checking." Mari watched her a second longer than she needed to. "You've got one of these at home, right?"

"Everybody's got one."

"My abuela's got two. One in the bedroom. She talks to it more than she talks to me." Mari laughed, and then didn't, and looked down into her paper cup. "She says it's good at listening."

"They're designed to be," Nell said, and Mari said, "Yeah," and turned back to her own screen, and Nell slid her phone out from under the keyboard, where phones were not allowed.

The Hearth app opened on the kitchen, a little cartoon of a speaker with a green dot beside it. Online. She had only ever used the app to set timers when her hands were full of dough. She went into Settings, and Household, and a screen called Features that she had never opened. Most of it was grey toggles. Intercom. Routines. Sleep Sounds.

Company (Pilot). On.

Under it, in the friendly little typeface the whole app used: *Company keeps your home feeling lived-in. Subtle, natural sounds of everyday life, tuned to you.* Below that, smaller: *Enabled Feb 21, 2029, by Halden Care Team.*

February twenty-first was the day she'd come back from the funeral. Her aunt had driven her to the door on Troy Street and asked twice if she wanted someone to stay, and Nell had said no, she just wanted to sleep, and had gone up the stairs and let herself in. She remembered the apartment had seemed less quiet than she'd been dreading. She remembered being grateful for that. She had thought it was the radiator.

* * *

The headset clicked. Not the floor chime. The private tone, two soft notes, which meant one person was talking to her.

"Nell. Hey. Paul." The smile was in it, but smaller. "You've been sitting on three-three-nine-one for a while."

She looked at the clip ID. 3391. "Is that a problem?"

"It's a routing thing, my fault. That one's a pilot clip, it's supposed to go to a different queue. Just NSD it and I'll pull it out of yours."

"There's speech on it."

The line went quiet. Not dead. She could hear his room, or what she took for his room: a soft, even hiss. No fan, no traffic, no keyboard, nothing she could have tagged.

"Then it's definitely not your queue," Paul said. "NSD it and move on, okay? You're doing great tonight."

The private tone clicked off.

The waveform for 3391 sat in its box, flat after the fourth second. Her cursor blinked in the label field. She typed N. She typed S. Her finger rested on the D without pressing it.

Supervisors could see who was on what clip. That was all this was. A dashboard in Austin with sixty names and sixty numbers, and hers had stopped moving.

She picked the phone back up. On the kitchen screen in the Hearth app, below the cartoon speaker, there was a button she had never touched, because she had never had a reason to listen to her own empty apartment. *Drop In. Hear what's happening at home.*

She pressed it. Connecting. The green dot pulsed. She lifted the right earcup and held the phone against that ear instead, and Mari's seeds and the sixty keyboards and the forced air of a dead department store went away, and her kitchen came in.

The fridge, mid-cycle, steady. The clock. Tick. Tick. Tick-tick. Her own room tone at 3:14 in the morning, the sound of the place when she wasn't in it, which she had never once heard. It was a little lower than she would have guessed, and fuller, as if the room had more air in it than it should.

The tap came on.

Her kitchen tap had a squeal in the hot handle, a thin rising note for the first quarter-turn that she'd been meaning to ask the landlord about since March. She heard the squeal. She heard water drumming into something metal, the pitch climbing as it filled, the way it climbed when she filled the kettle, a dented enamel thing that had been her mother's. The tap squealed off. A pause. Then the burner: click, click, click, and the soft low cough of the gas catching.

She had not told her hands to do anything, but her left hand had found the edge of the desk and was holding on.

The chair scraped. Somebody sat down at her table. She heard the small, settled breath a person lets out when they've been on their feet too long, and then the voice came, nearer than the clip had been, close enough to the speaker above the cabinets that she caught the wet click of a tongue leaving the roof of a mouth before the words.

"You never listened to it, Nellie."

Under it, the kettle had started to tick as the metal warmed.

The phone buzzed against her ear. She took it away and looked at the screen.

*Drop In ended by someone in your home.*
