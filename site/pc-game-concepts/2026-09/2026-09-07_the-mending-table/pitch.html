The Mending Table

Diagnose what's actually wrong with a stranger's broken keepsake, then choose a repair whose speed and materials trade off against how much of its history survives, before the next arrival crowds your bench.

Platform  PC (Windows, keyboard & mouse) Status  Concept Date  2026-09-07

Target player

Someone who finished Discounty or Coffee Talk and liked the shift work but wished the customers were more than a name and a request bubble. They've played Unpacking or PowerWash Simulator for the satisfaction of fixing one specific thing well, and they want that feeling stretched across a whole shop instead of one task. Not looking for Stardew's farm-empire scale — they want a small counter, a short queue, and a repair choice that actually meant something to the person who brought the object in.

Core loop

30-second loop

Inspect an object and every damage point lights up with the technique and material it needs — nothing hidden. Select a technique, drag the matching material, hold and release a short timing bar to apply it. Once every point is fixed, choose Full Restoration, which preserves the object's original character, or Quick Mend, which is faster and cheaper but visibly newer. That choice is supposed to match what the owner actually asked for.

30-minute loop

Three objects sit on the bench at once, each with a patience marker ambering toward its deadline. You triage which gets worked first, spend this week's limited peddler stock, and decide whether a rushed Quick Mend now is worth disappointing someone who clearly wanted the real thing. Finished pieces join the Keepsake Log and raise trust with whoever brought them in — trust that unlocks rarer materials and harder objects.

Standout systems

  • Inspect overlay & damage checklist

    Every damage point shows its technique, material, and time cost before you commit — nothing about a repair's cost is ever discovered mid-repair.

  • The authenticity/speed dial

    Each damage type has techniques that trade time and material cost against how much of the object's original character survives — the whole skill is reading which trade the customer actually wants.

  • Compound damage & contested keepsakes

    Later objects have damage points that fight each other, and a handful of objects per playthrough arrive with two claimants — the finish you choose is read by the town as taking a side.

  • Peddler cart & trust

    A weekly, limited-stock cart and a per-family trust score that gates rarer materials and harder objects — missed deadlines cost trust gently, never a hard fail.

Differentiators

  • The customer's want and the repair technique are the same decision, mechanically — not flavor text sitting next to the real gameplay, the way reviewers flagged Discounty's customer storylines for being.
  • Every cost is shown before you commit to it, answering the resource-visibility gap critics called out in Ember Folks, a comparable 2026 cozy-strategy entrant.
  • The objects are the town's own disaster history, and craftsmanship choices quietly take sides in it — contested keepsakes turn a repair into a relationship beat without a branching dialogue tree.

Key features

  • Inspect overlay shows every repair's cost before you commit — no hidden costs, ever
  • Authenticity-vs-speed dial makes the customer's want a real mechanical decision
  • Compound damage forces genuine trade-offs: fixing one flaw can ruin the ideal fix for another
  • Contested keepsakes let your craftsmanship quietly take a side in a small-town dispute
  • The Keepsake Log turns every finished repair into a personal scrapbook, built from real choices
  • No hard fail state — missed deadlines cost a small, recoverable trust dip, never a game over
