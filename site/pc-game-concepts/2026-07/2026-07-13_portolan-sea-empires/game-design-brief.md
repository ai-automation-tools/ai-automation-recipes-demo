# Portolan

> A maritime grand strategy spanning 1150–1600 CE where your empire is measured not in conquered land but in sea routes charted, port rights won, and commodities moved across the world's three great oceanic trade networks.

**Date**: 2026-07-13
**Genre**: grand strategy
**Platform**: PC (Windows; keyboard & mouse, controller optional)
**Status**: Concept

## Target player

The grand strategy player who has sunk hundreds of hours into Europa Universalis IV or Victoria 3 but finds the land-conquest treadmill stale — they want the same scope and depth but with economics and diplomacy as the primary engines, not army stacks. Also targets history enthusiasts frustrated by the genre's relentless Eurocentrism who want to command a Swahili sultanate or Majapahit sea-lord on equal mechanical footing with Venice. They are comfortable with complex interlocking systems but specifically want trade, information, and relationships to feel more meaningful than raw territory size.

## Core loop

**30-second loop:** Respond to a factor's dispatch from a distant port — a rival merchant is undercutting you on pepper prices in Calicut. Choose to match, extend credit to the port's ruler, or commission a privateer action against the competitor's next cargo ship.

**30-minute loop:** Open the Monsoon Calendar, confirm the southwest wind will allow your Malabar fleet to reach Hormuz in the next sailing window, negotiate a revised tariff treaty with the Omani harbor-master, then route a new commodity chain that turns Zanzibar ivory into Venetian ducats via three intermediate entrepôts — all before the October storms close the northern routes.

The game rewards players who read the world's rhythms (seasonal winds, court cycles, commodity demand curves) and position themselves ahead of rivals rather than forcing outcomes through military supremacy.

## Systems

- **Route-Network Map:** The strategic layer renders sea lanes, not land borders. You control connections (routes) between nodes (ports), not provinces. A port in your network is one where your faction holds trading rights — ranging from guest-trader status up to monopoly concession.
- **Monsoon Calendar:** Historical wind and current patterns open and close routes each season. The northeast monsoon blows October–April; the southwest monsoon June–September. Planning your commodity chains around real meteorological windows is the game's core tactical puzzle.
- **Factor Assignments:** Factors are your on-the-ground agents in foreign ports — they negotiate, bribe, spy, and manage your warehouses. Each factor has skills (Merchant, Diplomat, Navigator, Spy, Scholar) and a cultural background that affects how they are received in different ports. A Gujarati factor is trusted in Swahili ports; a Venetian factor is a foreigner who must earn standing.
- **Commodity Chains:** Forty real historical goods (pepper, cloves, silk, cotton, gold, ivory, porcelain, alum, horses, rhubarb) move along your routes. Prices are supply/demand driven. You profit from arbitrage — buy low in the producing region, sell high in the deficit region — and from controlling bottleneck ports.
- **Port Affinity & Politics:** Each port has a ruler, a merchant council, and a population. Your faction's Affinity score (0–100) determines access tier. Affinity rises through fair dealing, gifts, and shared faith; it falls through price gouging, piracy, and association with their enemies. You can never permanently "own" a foreign port — you can only cultivate trust or impose temporary concessions.
- **Rumor Network:** Information travels at sailing speed. News of a famine in Egypt, a new Sultan in Malacca, or a discovered route around Africa arrives weeks of in-game time after it happens. You can invest in a courier network to reduce the lag, or exploit information asymmetry against rivals who don't know what you know.
- **Military (Maritime Only):** You field no armies. Naval power exists to escort convoys, commission privateers against rivals, suppress piracy, and occasionally blockade a port that has broken a treaty. Battles are resolved quickly at the operational level; this is not the game's emphasis.
- **The Knowledge Map:** Your map fills in as your factors explore and as you purchase portolan charts from other navigators. Unknown regions are blank. Fully charting a route gives you first-mover advantage and tradeable knowledge (you can sell charts to other factions).

## Progression

**Early game (1150–1280):** Establish your home network, learn the monsoon rhythms, develop your first commodity chain, and recruit a diverse roster of factors. The world is relatively stable and the competition is establishing its footholds.

**Mid game (1280–1420):** The Mongol Pax opens overland routes that reshape demand for sea goods. The Black Death (1346) devastates European ports and reshuffles commodity prices globally. The rise of the Ottoman Empire changes the Mediterranean calculus. Responding to these external shocks — some predictable, some not — is the mid-game's main drama. This explicitly addresses the "midgame drag" design problem by making the mid-game the period of maximum systemic turbulence.

**Late game (1420–1600):** The Portuguese begin their southern route around Africa, threatening your Indian Ocean monopolies. Chinese treasure fleet expeditions redistribute power in Southeast Asian ports. The printing press spreads navigational knowledge, eroding your chart monopolies. The game's end is not a military climax but a trade-era transition — survival and adaptation define the finale.

**Victory conditions:** Commercial Supremacy (control 60% of a chosen commodity's total volume), The Great Chart (complete the world map — a Discovery victory), or Era Survival (hold your faction's historical core network through the arrival of the Portuguese/Ottomans and emerge from the transition era with sufficient wealth and influence to fund the next age).

**Meta-progression:** Between campaigns, unlock historical events, alternative faction starts, and specialist factor archetypes. A full playthrough takes 30–50 hours; a shortened "crisis" campaign (single historical event arc) plays in 10–15 hours.

## World & setting

Portolan covers the interconnected oceans of the medieval and early modern world from 1150 to 1600 CE — the Indian Ocean, the South China Sea, the Red Sea, the Persian Gulf, and the Mediterranean. Seven playable factions, each with distinct starting positions, cultural mechanics, and factional bonuses: the Republic of Venice, the Sultanate of Kilwa (Swahili Coast), the Merchants of Hormuz (Persian Gulf), the Majapahit Sea-Lords (Java), the Fujian Trade Guild (Song/Ming China), the Hanseatic League, and the Knights Hospitaller (Mediterranean naval power).

The art direction targets a hand-drawn illuminated manuscript aesthetic — the world map resembles a medieval portolan chart (compass roses, rhumb lines, coastlines rendered as colored vellum). Port cities are illustrated vignettes. The sea itself is alive: animated currents, seasonal wind arrows, and migrating storm bands communicate information at a glance without a dense UI layer. The color palette is warm — aged parchment, deep indigo sea, gold compass rose ink — with accents tuned to each faction's cultural register.

## Differentiators

- **Route-network territory:** Unlike every major grand strategy title, your empire is a graph of sea lanes and port relationships, not a contiguous land mass. Expansion means cultivating new trade access, not painting the map your color.
- **Monsoon-driven strategy:** Historical wind patterns are a hard mechanical constraint, not flavor. Seasonal route closures create genuine planning horizons that prevent the "always-aggressive" playstyle that dominates land-based grand strategy.
- **Pan-oceanic non-Eurocentric scope:** Kilwa, Hormuz, Majapahit, and Fujian are not minor-nation afterthoughts — they are mechanically unique, historically grounded, and fully playable with equal depth to Venice or the Hanseatic League.
- **Information as currency:** The portolan chart system makes navigational knowledge a tradeable strategic asset. Discovering and selling routes, buying intelligence, and exploiting information lag are distinct gameplay strategies unavailable in current genre titles.
- **No army-stack endgame:** The game cannot be won by military attrition. This makes it structurally distinct from EU5's war-or-diplomacy rhythm and specifically appeals to players who want economics to be the dominant strategy, not a preamble to conquest.

## Monetization notes

Premium release at $34.99 (competitive with Paradox's mid-tier titles and below EU5's $60). Early Access launch (12–18 months) with three playable factions (Venice, Kilwa, Majapahit), then 1.0 with all seven. Two DLC expansions planned post-launch: one adding the Malay Archipelago factions and detailed Southeast Asia routes, one adding a 1600–1700 epilogue covering the Dutch/English East India Company era — the logical historical next chapter. No cosmetic monetization; the portolan chart aesthetic does not lend itself to skin packs. The niche appeal to simulation-depth players and the historical novelty of the setting point toward a dedicated community with strong DLC attach rates, similar to how Victoria 3's unusual focus built a loyal base.

## Accessibility notes

- Fully remappable keyboard and controller bindings; designed to be completable keyboard-only or controller-only.
- Four colorblind-safe map palettes (Deuteranopia, Protanopia, Tritanopia, High-Contrast Monochrome) with route/faction colors legible in all modes.
- Scalable UI from 75% to 200%; all text passes WCAG AA contrast minimums at default scale.
- Pause-and-play at any time; no real-time pressure anywhere in the game.
- Adjustable game speed (1×–32× with a "pause on event" setting for players who want to process every dispatch at their own pace).
- Full subtitle/caption support for all voice content (faction advisor narration).
- No photosensitivity hazards: storm animations are slow and muted, no rapid flashes.
- A Guided Mode for new players reduces the factor roster, narrows the commodity list to eight goods, and marks optimal routes — turns the learning curve into a structured first campaign.

## Risks

- **Complexity vs. accessibility:** Maritime trade networks are harder to visualize than a land map; players used to the Paradox "paint the map" feedback loop may not immediately grasp the route-graph model. **Mitigation:** Guided Mode, an interactive tutorial that plays out one complete trade cycle, and a companion "factor's journal" that narrates what the player's choices meant in human terms.
- **Mid-game drag (the genre's known problem):** If external historical shocks are too rare or too predictable, the mid-game risks becoming routine route maintenance. **Mitigation:** A proceduralized event deck that randomizes timing within historical windows (the Black Death hits somewhere in 1340–1355), keeping players from pattern-memorizing the shock schedule.
- **Market size:** The maritime-trade niche is smaller than the Paradox historical wargame base. **Mitigation:** PC Game Pass inclusion at launch to maximize discovery; a shorter "Crisis Campaign" mode lowers the time commitment for first-time buyers.
- **Historical accuracy vs. playability:** Faithfully modeling monsoon closures and portolan-era navigation could feel punishing. **Mitigation:** Two simulation fidelity settings — Historical and Streamlined — toggle whether seasonal windows block routes hard or merely impose penalties.
- **Scope:** Seven factions across five ocean systems in 450 years is enormous. **Mitigation:** Three-faction Early Access launch; build the engine before adding content; design each ocean system as an independent module with shared infrastructure.

## 6-week prototype plan

- **Week 1:** Core route-network map — nodes (ports), edges (routes), a simple affinity score per port, and one commodity (pepper). No UI beyond debug overlays. Deliverable: one factor traveling one route and completing a trade cycle.
- **Week 2:** Monsoon Calendar system — encode historical wind tables, open/close route edges by season, add basic game-speed controls. Deliverable: the pepper route opening and closing correctly through a 12-month cycle.
- **Week 3:** Factor system — three factor archetypes (Merchant, Diplomat, Navigator), basic skill checks, dispatch/return travel time. Deliverable: a factor successfully negotiating an affinity increase at a foreign port.
- **Week 4:** Rival faction AI — one opponent faction using the same route-network model, competing for the same ports via its own factor assignments. Deliverable: observable AI behavior that competes for port affinity without cheating.
- **Week 5:** Commodity pricing — supply/demand model for five goods, arbitrage opportunity generation, player feedback on margin and price trends. Deliverable: a complete trade cycle with meaningful economic decisions.
- **Week 6:** Vertical-slice playtest — one full 50-year session playable as Venice vs. one AI Kilwa faction, with three commodities, the Monsoon Calendar, a working factor roster, a basic portolan chart reveal mechanic, and one scripted historical event (the Black Death). External playtest session with five target-player testers.
