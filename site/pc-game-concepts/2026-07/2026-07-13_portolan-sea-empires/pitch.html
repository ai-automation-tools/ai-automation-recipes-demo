PORTOLAN

Empires of the Sea · 1150–1600 CE

A maritime grand strategy spanning 1150–1600 CE where your empire is measured not in conquered land but in sea routes charted, port rights won, and commodities moved across the world's three great oceanic trade networks.

Grand Strategy PC (Windows) Keyboard & Mouse 450 Years of History
Who It's For
The Target Player

The grand strategy veteran who has sunk hundreds of hours into EU4 or Victoria 3 but finds the land-conquest treadmill stale. They want the same scope and depth — but with economics and diplomacy as the primary engines, not army stacks.

The Gap It Fills

History enthusiasts frustrated by the genre's relentless Eurocentrism who want to command a Swahili sultanate or Majapahit sea-lord on equal mechanical footing with Venice — with historically grounded systems, not flavour text.

Seven Playable Factions
Republic of Venice
Mediterranean
Masters of the galley trade and diplomatic cunning — start with the richest route network but face the most rivals.
Sultanate of Kilwa
Swahili Coast, East Africa
Control the gold and ivory entrepôt of the East African coast; uniquely positioned between interior kingdoms and the open Indian Ocean.
Merchants of Hormuz
Persian Gulf
Command the chokepoint of the Gulf — every ship passing between India and Arabia pays your toll or earns your enmity.
Majapahit Sea-Lords
Java, Southeast Asia
Rule the spice archipelago — cloves, nutmeg, and pepper all pass through your waters. Control the source, control the world's kitchens.
Fujian Trade Guild
Song / Ming China
Operate under the shadow of the imperial court — leverage the treasure fleet's wake while navigating the ever-shifting policies of the Son of Heaven.
Hanseatic League
Northern Europe
A merchant confederation, not a state — your power is legal and financial. Komtor houses in Bergen, Bruges, and Lübeck anchor a network no king can easily break.
Knights Hospitaller
Rhodes / Malta, Mediterranean
Naval power married to crusader politics — use your fleet to project force across the Mediterranean while managing the uncomfortable interests of your papal patrons.
Core Loop
30-Second Decision

A factor's dispatch arrives from Calicut — a rival is undercutting you on pepper. Match prices, extend credit to the port's ruler, or commission a privateer against the competitor's next cargo run. Every message is a triage call.

30-Minute Strategy

Open the Monsoon Calendar. The southwest wind opens the Malabar–Hormuz route in three weeks. Negotiate a tariff revision before the season closes. Route ivory from Zanzibar through three entrepôts to Venice. Every chain is an investment with a seasonal deadline.

Key Systems
Route-Network Map
The strategic layer renders sea lanes, not land borders. Your empire is a graph of connections — you compete for trading rights, not territory. A port in your network is one where your faction holds access ranging from guest-trader status to monopoly concession.
Monsoon Calendar
Historical wind and current patterns open and close routes each season. Planning supply chains around real meteorological windows is the game's core tactical puzzle — you cannot force the wind.
Factor Assignments
Factors are your on-the-ground agents in foreign ports. Each has skills (Merchant, Diplomat, Navigator, Spy, Scholar) and a cultural background that affects reception. A Gujarati factor is trusted in Swahili ports; a Venetian is a foreigner who must earn standing.
Commodity Chains
Forty real historical goods move along your routes on a supply/demand model. Profit from arbitrage — buy low at the source, sell high in the deficit region — and from controlling bottleneck ports where every commodity must transit.
Rumor Network
Information travels at sailing speed. News of a famine in Egypt or a new Sultan in Malacca arrives weeks after it happens. You can invest in a courier network to reduce lag — or exploit information asymmetry against rivals who don't know what you know yet.
The Knowledge Map
Your map fills in as your factors explore and as you purchase portolan charts from other navigators. Unknown regions are blank. Fully charting a route gives you first-mover advantage and tradeable knowledge — navigational intelligence as hard currency.
Key Features
◆ Route-network empire building — your territory is sea lanes and port relationships, not a contiguous land mass
◆ Monsoon Calendar — historical wind patterns open and close routes each season, forcing real planning horizons
◆ Seven playable factions spanning Venice to Majapahit — each grounded in historical source material, none a reskin of another
◆ Forty historical commodities (pepper, cloves, silk, gold, ivory) on a supply/demand economy — win through arbitrage, not attrition
◆ Rumor Network — information travels at sailing speed; exploit intelligence lag before rivals learn what you already know
◆ Portolan chart discovery — map the world's sea lanes as tradeable strategic assets; sell navigational knowledge to fund your next expansion
◆ Mid-game historical shocks (Mongol Pax, Black Death, Ottoman expansion, Portuguese arrival) that procedurally randomize timing — no two playthroughs read the same
◆ Illuminated manuscript art direction — the strategic map renders as a medieval portolan chart, with compass roses, rhumb lines, and animated seasonal winds
What Sets It Apart
01
Route-Network Territory
Unlike every major grand strategy title, your empire is a graph of sea lanes and port access rights — not a painted land mass. Expansion means cultivating trust and trade access, not conquering provinces.
02
Monsoon-Driven Strategy
Historical wind patterns are a hard mechanical constraint, not flavor text. Seasonal route closures create genuine planning horizons that prevent the always-aggressive playstyle that dominates land-based grand strategy.
03
Non-Eurocentric by Design
Kilwa, Hormuz, Majapahit, and Fujian are not minor-nation afterthoughts — they are mechanically unique, historically grounded, and fully playable with equal system depth to Venice or the Hanseatic League.
04
Information as Currency
Portolan charts and the rumor network make navigational knowledge a distinct strategic asset. Discovering and selling routes, buying intelligence, and exploiting information lag are strategies unavailable in current genre titles.
05
No Army-Stack Endgame
The game cannot be won by military attrition. Economics is the dominant strategy, not a preamble to conquest — a structural distinction from EU5's war-or-diplomacy rhythm that defines a different player experience entirely.
Campaign Arc
1150–
1280
Early Game — The Stable Sea
Establish your home network, learn the monsoon rhythms, build your first commodity chains, and recruit a diverse roster of factors. Competition is fierce but the world is stable — learn by doing before the shocks arrive.
1280–
1420
Mid Game — Age of Disruption
The Mongol Pax reshapes overland routes; the Black Death (1340–1355, randomized) devastates European ports and reshuffles commodity prices globally; the Ottoman Empire changes the Mediterranean calculus. Responding to these external shocks — some predictable, some not — is the game's main drama.
1420–
1600
Late Game — Era Transition
The Portuguese begin their southern route around Africa. Ming treasure fleet expeditions redistribute power in Southeast Asian ports. The printing press spreads navigational knowledge, eroding your chart monopolies. The end is a trade-era transition — survival and adaptation, not a military climax.
