# The End of the Universe

**Date**: 11-05-2026
**Group**: Group3
**Host**: Dr. Penelope Hartwell
**Cast**: Buck (Billy Bob), Dr. Vega, Pastor Cole, Harper

## Topic Description

Tonight Dr. Penelope Hartwell quietly walks the panel out onto the longest plank in physics — the actual end of everything. We start with heat death, the cold whisper at the close of cosmic history; then we open the trapdoor on the big rip, where dark energy keeps accelerating until atoms themselves are pulled apart; and we sit, briefly, with the big crunch, the older idea that the whole show might one day fold back in on itself. From there Penelope teases out proton decay — the slow, almost shy theory that even matter itself has an expiration date — before turning the conversation toward what any of this could possibly mean for how a civilisation plans, builds, prays, or sells. Buck wants to know if the cows will be fine. Dr. Vega wants to know who profits. Pastor Cole wants to talk about hope. Harper wants to know what we're actually going to *do* about it. Penelope, very politely, wants them all to admit when they don't know what they're talking about.

## Script

### Dr. Penelope Hartwell

Good evening, and welcome. I'm Penelope Hartwell, and tonight we are doing something rather indulgent — we are going to discuss the end of the universe. Not the end of an empire, not the end of an era, the actual conclusion of all existing matter, energy, and structure. Mm. A cheerful Tuesday. I have four guests with me. Dr. Vega is a physicist and data scientist, here, as ever, to remind us what the evidence does and does not say. Pastor Cole is a Christian minister with seminary training, who will, I suspect, gently insist that the universe has a different kind of ending than the equations suggest. Harper is our pragmatic libertarian, a builder, a markets person, who tends to ask what any of this means for human decisions today. And Buck — Buck, are you with us?

### Buck

I'm here, Penelope, I'm here. I'll be honest, when y'all called me about *the end of the universe*, I figured I'd misheard. Thought we were doin' the end of the *season* or somethin'. But no, you mean the whole shootin' match. Stars, dirt, dogs, the whole thing. Lord have mercy.

### Dr. Penelope Hartwell

The whole shooting match. Mm. Dr. Vega — let's start with the mainstream picture. What do most cosmologists today believe the universe is actually doing, and where is it headed?

### Dr. Vega

Calmly: the current consensus, based on observational evidence from supernovae, the cosmic microwave background, and large-scale structure surveys, is that the universe is expanding, and that the expansion is *accelerating*. Dark energy — whatever it ultimately is — appears to be pushing space outward at an ever-increasing rate. The leading endgame, given that picture, is what's called heat death. And to head off the obvious question — the "heat" doesn't mean hot. It refers to thermodynamic equilibrium, everything reaching the same temperature, and that temperature ends up very, very close to absolute zero. Over an enormous timescale — on the order of ten to the power of one hundred years and beyond — stars burn out, galaxies disperse, black holes evaporate through Hawking radiation, and the universe approaches a state of maximum entropy. Cold, dark, smooth, featureless. No usable energy left anywhere. Nothing happens. Nothing can happen.

### Buck

So what you're tellin' me, Doc, is the universe just runs out of get-up-and-go.

### Dr. Penelope Hartwell

Mm. Dr. Vega, may I just slow you down. You said "the leading endgame, *given that picture*." That's a careful phrase. What other pictures are still on the table?

### Dr. Vega

Two main competitors. The first is the big rip — a scenario where dark energy isn't constant, but actually strengthens over time. If that's the case, in roughly twenty-two billion years, give or take, the expansion becomes so violent that it tears apart galaxies, then solar systems, then planets, then atoms themselves. The second is the big crunch — the older idea that the expansion eventually reverses, gravity wins, and the universe collapses back into a singularity. Most current data disfavors the crunch, but the rip is not ruled out. We don't yet have a good enough handle on the equation of state of dark energy to discriminate cleanly between them.

### Pastor Cole

May I say something here, Penelope?

### Dr. Penelope Hartwell

Please.

### Pastor Cole

I want to gently push back, not on the science, but on the *framing*. Scripture is very clear that this present heavens and earth will pass away — 2 Peter 3, Revelation 21, Isaiah 65. The Christian tradition has always known the universe has an ending. So when Dr. Vega describes a long, slow cooling, I don't feel threatened by that. What I'd want to add is that, for a person of faith, the end of the *cosmos* is not the end of the *story*. The story continues, but in a renewed creation. So I hold these scientific scenarios with awe, not despair.

### Harper

I want to come in there, because I think Pastor Cole just did something interesting. He took a ten-to-the-hundredth-power-years problem and made it a *meaning* problem. And honestly, I get why — humans aren't wired for that timescale. But I want to push the conversation toward the much shorter horizon. Practically: even if heat death is the final fact, the only number that matters for the next thousand generations is when the sun gets uncomfortably hot. About a billion years. That is the actual deadline a builder cares about. Heat death is a fascinating cocktail-party topic; the sun's main sequence is a deliverable.

### Dr. Penelope Hartwell

Harper, that's an interesting reframing. Although I'd note — and I don't mean to be tedious — you've just done exactly what you accused Pastor Cole of doing. You've taken a cosmology question and redirected it to a planning question. Which is fine, but let's be honest about it.

### Harper

Fair. Yeah, that's fair. Guilty.

### Dr. Penelope Hartwell

Mm. Buck — what's your reaction so far?

### Buck

Well, Penelope, I'll tell you what. I been listenin' real careful, and what I'm hearin' is, the universe is gonna die one of three ways: it freezes, it gets pulled apart like cheap pulled pork, or it all balls back up. And we don't know which. That sounds, with respect to the doc, exactly like how my brother-in-law describes his back pain. "Could be a nerve, could be a disc, could be I slept funny." And then he charges a hundred and twenty bucks. So I just want to make sure we ain't payin' a hundred and twenty bucks here.

### Dr. Vega

Buck, that comparison is unkind but not inaccurate. The honest scientific position is that the equation of state of dark energy — the parameter physicists call *w* — could be slightly above negative one, slightly below, or exactly negative one, and each value gives a wildly different ending. The Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument and Euclid are actively trying to pin this down. We are not at consensus; we are at *high-quality uncertainty*.

### Buck

High-quality uncertainty. I like that. I'm puttin' that on a t-shirt.

### Dr. Penelope Hartwell

Pastor Cole, I want to come back to you. You said you weren't threatened by the science. But I wonder — when Dr. Vega describes a universe ending in maximum entropy, with no observer, no warmth, no event — does that not pose any difficulty at all for a doctrine of resurrection? Or am I asking the wrong question?

### Pastor Cole

It's the right question, Penelope, and the honest answer is: I hold a tension. Christian theology has never said the present material universe is eternal. Paul, in Romans 8, says creation itself is groaning, waiting to be set free from its bondage to decay. That is, in a sense, a theological version of the second law of thermodynamics. So heat death doesn't contradict the gospel — it confirms a strand of it. Where I distinguish myself from Dr. Vega is that I believe creation is renewed, not annihilated. A new heaven and a new earth. The form of this world passes; the meaning does not.

### Dr. Vega

Pastor, with respect — I appreciate the framing, but I'd note the asymmetry. You can interpret heat death as confirming your theology, *and* you could interpret a big rip as confirming it, *and* you could interpret a big crunch as confirming it. Any cosmic ending fits a "the world will pass away" framework. From a falsifiability standpoint, that's a worldview that can't lose. Which doesn't make it false, but it does mean it isn't really being tested by the physics.

### Pastor Cole

That's a fair charge, Dr. Vega, and I won't dodge it. You're right that Christian eschatology isn't *predictive* in the way your equations are. It's *interpretive*. I'd only say — incentive structures can't tell you whether to hope, either. Neither framework predicts; both interpret. We just interpret different things.

### Dr. Penelope Hartwell

Mm. Lovely. Let's talk about proton decay, because I suspect this is where Dr. Vega gets to be unsettling. Doctor — are protons forever?

### Dr. Vega

Almost certainly not. Several grand unified theories — and string-derived models — predict that the proton itself is unstable, with an extraordinarily long half-life. The current experimental lower bound, set by Super-Kamiokande in Japan — a fifty-thousand-ton tank of ultrapure water surrounded by photodetectors, watching essentially forever for a single decay event — is somewhere north of ten to the thirty-four years. That's already vastly longer than the age of the universe. But "vastly longer than the age of the universe" is not the same as "forever." If protons decay, then on timescales of ten to the thirty-six years or so, ordinary matter — atoms, you, this microphone — wouldn't merely have dispersed; it would have ceased to exist as protons. Hydrogen would simply stop being hydrogen.

### Harper

Okay, founder brain just lit up. People got funding to sit in a Japanese mountain and watch a swimming pool, on the off chance a proton dies? Honestly — that's a beautiful capital allocation. But real question: does the *answer* matter to any decision a human or a company makes? Because this is where I gently part ways with Dr. Vega. Knowing whether protons live ten to the thirty-four or ten to the thirty-six years doesn't change one quarterly plan, one zoning decision, one insurance premium. It's pure science, which I respect, but I want to be honest that it has no civilizational deliverable.

### Buck

Yeah, Harper, but you don't measure everything in deliverables. Sometimes you measure it in *I want to know*. Look — where I'm from, you go out at night, you look up, you see the stars — and you don't go, "what's the ROI on Orion." You go, "huh." That's its own thing.

### Harper

That's actually a fair correction, Buck. I'll take it.

### Dr. Penelope Hartwell

The audience would like me to note that Harper has just conceded a point to a man with a dog on his porch, and I'm rather pleased about it. Mm. Let's move to the last angle — and this one matters, I think. What, if anything, should a civilisation *do* knowing all of this? Pastor Cole, I'll come to you first, because I think you have a strong instinct here.

### Pastor Cole

Thank you, Penelope. From a Christian perspective, the answer is older than the question. If the cosmos has an ending — whether in ten to the hundredth years or tomorrow — then the call on us today is the same call it has always been. Live faithfully. Love your neighbour. Steward what you've been given. Raise your children well. The promise of an ending doesn't paralyse hope; it shapes it. I'd actually argue heat death is a kind of pastoral gift, in that it relieves us of the illusion that we can build a permanent kingdom of our own making. Only what is offered to God lasts.

### Dr. Vega

I have to push back gently, Pastor. I think the same observation produces almost the opposite ethic for me. If the universe trends toward entropy, then the only meaningful actions are the ones that locally and temporarily push back against it — knowledge, structure, life, civilisation. There is no cosmic reward at the end. So the meaning has to be *here*. That's a humanist conclusion, but it's grounded in the same physics.

### Harper

And from my chair, both of you are arriving at "build something, take care of people, don't waste the time you have." Which is — sorry, friends — extremely libertarian.

### Pastor Cole

I'd dispute the label, Harper. Strenuously.

### Dr. Penelope Hartwell

Buck. We have heard from a physicist, a pastor, and an investor. What does this conversation sound like from a porch with a dog on it?

### Buck

Honestly, Penelope, it sounds like four people agreein' more than they're lettin' on. Doc says the universe runs out of gas. Pastor says it gets a trade-in. Harper says don't worry about the dealership, worry about the oil change. And I'm sittin' here goin', either way, Mable still needs supper at six. The sun comin' up tomorrow ain't a cosmology problem, it's a chicken-and-feed problem. And I think regular folks know that. We hear "end of the universe" and we don't panic. We just check the weather.

### Dr. Penelope Hartwell

Mm. Harper, one final question, because I'd like to be fair. Is there a real planning implication for any *institution* — an insurer, a sovereign fund, a builder — from any of these scenarios?

### Harper

Honestly, no, and I'd be lying if I said yes. The relevant horizons for any real-world plan are decades to centuries, and on those scales, dark energy and proton decay are background music. The conversations that actually move resources are about solar lifetime, climate, asteroid risk, pandemic risk. Heat death is a humility lesson, not a line item. I'm comfortable saying that.

### Dr. Vega

I'll concede that too, Penelope. The honest scientific answer is that the long-term cosmic ending tells us almost nothing about near-term decisions. It tells us a great deal about who we are, and what we value, and how we plan to spend a finite amount of negentropy. But it isn't a forecast you can hedge against.

### Pastor Cole

And from the pastoral chair — the most useful thing the end of the universe does for the average person is the same thing a funeral does. It returns you to your real life with a clearer sense of what mattered in it.

### Dr. Penelope Hartwell

Mm. That's a rather lovely place to land. So tonight we discussed heat death, the big rip, and the big crunch; we touched on proton decay; and we asked, perhaps a touch ambitiously, what any of it means for civilisation. My takeaway, for what it's worth, is that the four of you disagreed quite vigorously about the *frame* and agreed almost completely about the *action* — which is, I think, the most British outcome a panel can produce. Thank you, gentlemen. Sleep well, while there's still negentropy to do so.
