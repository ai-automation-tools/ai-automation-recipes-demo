# The Spacecraft Lost to a Unit Label

**Category**: Expensive Mistakes
**Topic**: NASA Mars Climate Orbiter Loss (1999)
**Length**: 98 seconds (~261 words)

---

September twenty-third, 1999. NASA's Mars Climate Orbiter fires its main engine to drop into orbit around Mars. Nine months of flight come down to this one burn.

Then the spacecraft swings behind the planet, exactly as planned, and mission control waits for its signal to come back.

The plan said twenty-one minutes. The signal never returns. It had actually cut out forty-nine seconds early, and nobody knew yet what that meant.

Here is what the investigators found. The navigators had aimed the orbiter to pass two hundred twenty-six kilometers above Mars. The spacecraft could survive down to eighty. The best reconstruction put it at fifty-seven. It went too deep into the atmosphere, and that was the end of it.

The cause was a units mix-up. A ground software file called Small Forces calculated the push from the spacecraft's thrusters. The contract said the numbers should come out in newton-seconds. The software gave pound-seconds instead.

One pound of force is four point four five newtons. So every little thruster firing looked more than four times weaker to the navigators than it really was. Small errors, stacked up across nine months, bent the path toward Mars.

And people noticed something was off. Navigators had been talking with the spacecraft engineers about odd discrepancies in the numbers. The units problem was found on September twenty-ninth. Six days after the orbiter was already gone.

The whole project cost about three hundred twenty-eight million dollars, counting launch and operations.

The orbiter crossed the solar system, hit its timing, and lost to a label. Pounds, or newtons.
