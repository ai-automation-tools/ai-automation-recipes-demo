# The Warship That Sank Before It Left the Harbor

**Category**: Expensive Mistakes
**Topic**: The Sinking of the Swedish Warship Vasa (1628)
**Length**: 135 seconds (~356 words)
**Read by**: voiceover narrator (third person)

---

Sweden's brand new warship sinks about thirteen hundred meters into its first voyage. Barely a hundred and twenty meters from shore.

August 10, 1628. A Sunday afternoon in Stockholm. The Vasa slips its moorings below the royal palace and fires a salute. Sixty-four guns, stacked on two gun decks. It belongs to King Gustav II Adolf, who is away fighting a war in Poland and wants his ship at sea.

Four of its ten sails are up. A breeze catches them and the ship leans. It rights itself. Then, off Tegelviken, a harder gust hits. The Vasa rolls onto its port side. The lower gunports are open. Water pours in.

Within minutes it's on the bottom, thirty-two meters down, masts still poking out of the water. About thirty people die. Most of them are trapped inside the hull.

And somebody already knew.

Before it ever sailed, the captain staged a test for Vice Admiral Klas Fleming. Thirty men ran back and forth across the upper deck to see how far the ship would rock. After three runs, Fleming stopped it. He was afraid the ship would capsize right there.

A crewman later testified that Fleming said, "If only His Majesty were at home!" Nobody sent word to the king. The ship sailed anyway.

The flaw was simple. Too much ship above the waterline, not enough below it. The center of gravity sat so high that a light wind could tip it.

So the Council of State held an inquest. They jailed the captain. He swore the crew was sober and the guns were lashed down. The builders said they'd followed plans the king himself approved. The original shipwright, Henrik Hybertsson, had died in 1627. The blame drifted onto a dead man.

Fleming was never even questioned. Nobody was punished. The men who were questioned went on to be promoted.

The Vasa just sat there. The cold, brackish Baltic has no shipworm to eat the wood. In 1961, three hundred thirty-three years later, they raised it almost whole. More than forty-five million people have since walked around it.

Sweden's most famous warship never made it one mile.
