# The Blue Words

The firmware update took eleven minutes, and Dee at the Costco hearing center on Miller Hill spent them explaining it to him twice.

"The federal people made them add it," she said. She had his left aid sitting in a little plastic cradle wired to her laptop, and she kept glancing at the progress bar like it might wander off. "Anything the aid doesn't actually catch, it guesses. That's always been true. Now it has to tell you."

"Guesses," Walt said.

"Fills in. Somebody turns their head, a truck goes by, they mumble. It takes what it did catch and finishes the sentence. It's been doing it since the day you got them, Walt. You never noticed. That's the whole point of it."

"How much?"

She pulled up his profile. "For you, about eleven percent. That's normal. I've got people at twenty."

He thought about that on the drive home down the hill, the lake spread out flat and pewter below him, the lift bridge standing up at the end of Canal Park like a goalpost. Eleven percent. One word in nine. He'd worked thirty-one years on the ore docks for the railroad, which was where the ears went, and in all that time he had never once let a man finish his sentences for him. Now a piece of plastic the size of a lima bean had been doing it for three years and the government had to make it confess.

* * *

Ren had set his phone up with the big fonts, so the app looked like a children's book. There was a new tab at the bottom called HISTORY. He sat at the kitchen table with his coffee and opened it.

It was everything. Every sentence anyone had said to him since the spring he got the aids, stacked up in a column that went down and down when he dragged his thumb. Most of it was gray. Here and there a word or a phrase was blue, and when he pressed on a blue word a little box came up that said FILLED and a percentage.

He started with the easy ones. The girl at the Super One checkout, last Tuesday: *Have a good one.* "One" was blue, ninety-four percent. Fair enough. Everybody said have a good one. If you caught "have a good" you could bet the house.

Gene at the Legion, Thursday, talking about his grandson's hockey: *He's got hands like you wouldn't believe, but he skates like a* and then blue, *drunk giraffe*, sixty-one percent.

Walt laughed out loud in the empty kitchen. He picked up the house phone and called Gene.

"Thursday. Your grandson. He skates like a what?"

"What?"

"You said he skates like a something. What'd you say?"

There was a pause while Gene, who was eighty, went back four days. "Like a shopping cart," Gene said. "One bad wheel. Why?"

"No reason," Walt said, and hung up, and sat there feeling a little sick, though he couldn't have said why. Drunk giraffe was funnier. Drunk giraffe was what he'd have said himself.

That was the thing, he worked out over the next couple of hours, scrolling. The aid didn't fill in what you'd said. It filled in what people said. It took the front half of your sentence and gave it the back half that most people, most of the time, put on it. When the weatherman on WDIO turned away from the camera, the aid finished his forecast with *partly cloudy* because forecasts end in partly cloudy. When the pharmacist said *Take one in the morning with*, the aid said *food*. Usually that was right. That was why it worked. You could go three years and never notice, because most of what anybody says is what anybody would say.

He kept scrolling. He knew what he was looking for, and he went the long way around to it anyway, through the summer and the spring, through a whole year of Gene and the Super One and the Lutheran pastor who mumbled into his chest. He went past the funeral. He didn't look at the funeral.

Then he was in February, two years back, and he found it.

* * *

Marlys had said she was tired after supper. That was the start of it. She'd done the dishes and then she'd sat down on the couch instead of in her chair, which he noticed and didn't think about, and when he asked if she wanted the news on she said something with a wrong shape to it. Not slurred, exactly. Like her mouth was a size off.

He'd called Ren. That was the part he never told anybody. Not Gene, not the pastor, not the grief group at the Hillside library that he went to twice and quit. He'd stood in the kitchen with the cord stretched out so he could see Marlys through the doorway, and he called his daughter, who was an ER nurse at St. Luke's and was on shift, and she'd asked him the questions. Could Mom lift both arms. Was her face even. Did she know what day it was.

Marlys lifted both arms. Her face looked all right to him. She knew it was Wednesday.

*She's exhausted, Dad,* Ren had said. *She was up half the night with that cough. Check her sugar if you want. Let her sleep. I'll come by after I'm off.*

So he let her sleep. He put the afghan over her. In the morning she couldn't get up, and her left hand wouldn't close, and the man in the ambulance used the word *hours* in a voice that meant too many of them. She lived three days at St. Mary's. She never said anything else that had the right shape.

Two weeks after the funeral, Ren had come over and they'd sat out on the front porch in their coats, because the house was too full of casseroles and Marlys. It was maybe twenty degrees. You could see the whole harbor from that porch, the elevators and the frozen slips and the bridge, and Ren sat on the top step with her back half to him, looking at all of it. She'd been crying on and off since she got there. A city plow came grinding down East Fifth with its blade throwing sparks off the pavement, and while it went past she said the thing.

*You did everything right, Dad. You couldn't have known.*

He had held on to that sentence for two years. He'd held on to it in the shower and in the truck and at three in the morning when he woke up sure he could hear Marlys breathing in the other room. He'd said it to himself the way Marlys used to say the Lord's Prayer at the kitchen sink, not for the words so much as the rhythm. You did everything right. You couldn't have known. His daughter was a nurse. She knew what a stroke looked like. If she said he couldn't have known, then he couldn't have.

On the phone screen it said:

*You did* in gray.

*everything right, Dad. You couldn't have known.* in blue.

He pressed on it. FILLED, the box said. 38%.

* * *

He did not sleep that night. He lay on his back with the aids out on the nightstand in their little charging case, glowing green, and the house was as quiet as the inside of a fist.

Thirty-eight percent. He'd worked out by then what the number meant, more or less. It meant the aid had caught two words and a plow, and it had gone looking through everything it knew about what a grown daughter says to her father two weeks after her mother dies, and it had come back with the likeliest thing. The kindest thing, maybe, though he doubted the machine knew the difference. What a daughter says in a movie.

And it was only thirty-eight percent sure. Which meant that sixty-two times out of a hundred, she'd said something else.

*You did.* That was what he had for certain. Two words. You did. You did this. You did nothing. You did what? You didn't call 911, you didn't look close enough, you sat in your recliner watching the Wild lose to Winnipeg while her brain went dark a piece at a time ten feet away. You did.

He thought about the way Ren had been since then. Not cold. She came every other Sunday and called on Wednesdays. She fixed things on his phone. But she didn't stay long, and she didn't look at him much, and she never, ever came out to the porch anymore. He'd figured it was grief. People grieve sideways. Now he lay in the dark and wondered if it was the other thing. If she'd said *You did this, Dad,* plain as day, and he'd heard *You did everything right,* and he had said, he remembered this exactly, he'd said *I know, honey. I know.*

What would that have sounded like to her? Your father, two weeks after, nodding and saying I know.

At four-thirty he got up and put the aids back in and went out on the porch in his robe. The lake was black. There was one freighter at anchor off the piers, waiting on the morning, all its deck lights on, and it looked like a little town that had floated away from somewhere.

* * *

Dee couldn't help him. He went back up to Miller Hill on Monday and stood at her counter while a woman behind him in line sighed every forty seconds.

"There's no audio, Walt. It never keeps the sound. Privacy rules. It keeps the text so it can learn your voices, and that's it."

"So whatever she actually said."

"It's gone. It was gone the second it happened. The aid only ever had the two words." She looked at him, and something in her face changed, and she lowered her voice. "Is this about your wife?"

"No," he said. "It's about my daughter."

"Then ask your daughter."

He drove home the long way, down London Road along the water, past the rose garden with all the beds cut back and mulched for winter. *Ask your daughter.* Like you could. Like you could say to your only child, hey, two years ago on the porch, while the plow went by, did you tell me I killed your mother? Because I didn't catch it.

And if she said yes. If she said, Dad, I've been waiting two years for you to say something back besides I know.

Or worse, if she didn't remember. If whatever she'd said had gone out of her the way it had gone out of the aid, and all that was left was what he'd answered.

On Wednesday she called like always. He watched the words come up on the phone as she talked, gray, gray, gray, a blue *tomorrow* where the line crackled. She was working a double. Somebody in the department had quit. The Subaru needed tires. She'd come Sunday.

"Ren," he said.

"Yeah?"

He looked out the kitchen window at the porch. "Bring a heavier coat Sunday," he said. "I want to sit outside."

There was a gap on the line. On the screen, nothing came up, gray or blue. Then she said okay, and she said it in gray.

* * *

Before she came, he went into the app and found the switch. It was under SETTINGS, then LISTENING, then a line that said PREDICTIVE FILL with a little green toggle next to it and a paragraph of warning underneath in the big font. *Turning off Predictive Fill may significantly reduce speech clarity in noisy environments. Most users prefer to leave this setting on.*

He turned it off.

It was like somebody had pulled the plugs out of the world. Not quieter. Rougher. The refrigerator had a rattle in it he'd never heard. The radio in the kitchen, which he'd left on low to WDSE, became a man saying half of words, *—lowing a brief —sage from our —sors.* When he said "Testing" out loud to himself, his own voice came back to him with the end clipped off.

He left it off. He sat at the table and listened to the radio for an hour, the whole broken hour of it, until he could start to hear the shapes again under the gaps. It was like the docks in his first year, before he learned to read the foremen's lips through the noise of the loaders. You didn't get everything. You got enough if you watched.

* * *

Ren came at two on Sunday with a pan of bars and her good parka, the green one Marlys had bought her at Fitger's three Christmases ago. She was forty-four. She had her mother's way of standing with one hip cocked like she'd only stopped for a second.

"It's forty degrees, Dad."

"I know it."

"You want to sit out in forty degrees."

"Humor me."

They went out. He took his chair and she went for the top step, the same as before, with her back half turned to him, looking out at the harbor. October, so the shipping was running. There were two salties in the anchorage and a laker coming down from the ore docks low in the water, riding heavy, the way he liked to see them.

"Sit here," he said. He pulled the other chair around. Marlys's chair. "Where I can see you."

She looked at the chair a second. Then she sat in it.

He'd rehearsed something. He'd rehearsed it all week. He didn't use any of it.

"These hearing aids," he said. "They got an update. Turns out they've been making things up."

"What?"

"When they don't catch something. They guess. Then they let you hear the guess like it's real. Now they have to show you which parts they guessed." He got out his phone and found the line, though he could have recited it, and he held it out to her. "That's you. On the porch. After."

She took the phone and read it, and he watched her read it. He watched the words go into her. *You did* in gray. The rest in blue.

"Is that what you heard?" she said. "That's what you heard me say?"

"Thirty-eight percent," he said. "The machine wasn't sure. It's been a long time, honey, and I wouldn't ask, except I been carrying it. I need to know what you said."

She was very still. A gull went over. Down on the water the laker was swinging out toward the canal, and he saw the bridge start to lift for it, the whole road deck rising up between the towers, slow as anything.

"I said," Ren started, and stopped.

He leaned in. Without the fill her voice came to him with pieces missing, the edges of words fuzzed off like a radio station at the far end of the dial. He watched her mouth.

"I said, you did what I told you to." She was looking at the phone, not at him. "I told you to let her sleep. I'm a nurse. I'm a nurse, Dad, I do this all day, I ask the questions and I listen to the answers, and I heard you on the phone and I heard Mom in the background and I didn't hear it. I was tired. I had a guy coding in bay four and I wanted to get off the phone. So I told you let her sleep, and you did what I told you to." She put the phone face down on her knee. "That's what I said. And you said, I know."

"I didn't hear it."

"You said I know. You said, I know, honey." Her voice went out on him there, the aid getting nothing, and he watched her lips and got the rest. "I've been waiting two years for the rest of that sentence."

He sat back. The chair creaked. He thought about the column on the phone, gray and blue, and how he'd gone looking through it like a man going through a dead man's pockets, sure of what he'd find.

All this time. All this time, she had been sitting in the other half of the same sentence.

"I thought you said I did everything right," he said. "I thought my daughter the nurse said I couldn't have known. I been living on that. Two years. I got up in the morning on that."

"Dad."

"No, listen to me. I'm not done." His voice was coming back to him short, the ends clipped, and he talked slower so he could hear it. "I never told anybody I called you first. Not Gene, not Pastor Lindahl, nobody. You know why? Because it wasn't your fault, and I didn't want anybody even thinking it. You were at work. You had a man dying in bay four. You asked me could she lift her arms and she lifted her arms. You asked me was her face even and I looked and I said yes. I was the one in the room. I was the one who could see her."

Ren had her hand over her mouth.

"That machine," he said. "It didn't know what you said. It just knew what people say. It gave me the likeliest thing. And I took it, because I wanted it." He reached over and took the phone off her knee and set it on the porch boards, face down, out of the way. "It was a good sentence, though. It just come to the wrong house." He waited until she looked at him. "So here. You did everything right, Ren. You couldn't have known."

The laker was in the canal now, sliding between the piers, and the captain blew for the bridge. The sound came rolling up the hill the way it always had, big enough to lean on, and it went right through the middle of whatever Ren was saying back to him. He saw her mouth move. He didn't get any of it.

Three years he'd had a little machine that would have handed him something there. Something warm and likely. He could feel the shape of the gap where it would have gone.

"Again," he said. "Say it again. I didn't catch it."

She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, the way her mother used to, and she leaned in close so the gaps couldn't get her, and she said it again.

He got all of it.
