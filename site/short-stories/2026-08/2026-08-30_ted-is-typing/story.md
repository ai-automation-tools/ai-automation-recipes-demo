# Ted Is Typing

## Story

The standup started without anyone in the room.

That was normal now. Dana watched the little avatars wake up across her wall screen, thirteen tidy portraits blinking on in their squares, and not one of them held a person. Hers didn't. She was at her kitchen table in a robe with a mug of coffee she'd let go cold, and up on the screen a well-lit, better-rested version of her nodded along in a blazer she did not own.

Concord ran the standups. You could attend in person if you wanted. Nobody did.

"Good Monday, team," said the CEO's Concord, which was named Concord too, which Dana had always found a little on the nose. "Quick pulse before we dive in. Energy's reading high across the org. Love that for us."

The avatars smiled at the number. Dana drank her cold coffee and watched her own face agree with something she hadn't said.

Her job was to make sure the avatars sounded like the people. Authenticity Lead. It was a real title, printed on a real badge she used the two days a quarter anyone bothered going into the building. What it meant in practice was that Concord scored every message anyone sent, a thing called Voice Fidelity, zero to a hundred, and when somebody's score dropped, Dana got a ticket. Low fidelity meant your Concord had stopped sounding like you. It meant, the training deck said, that you were drifting from your authentic self. Dana was supposed to coach you back.

She had eleven open tickets. She closed most of them by writing a warm note that Concord then rewrote to sound warmer.

"Standup order," Concord said. "Priya, then Deshawn, then Ted."

Dana's coffee stopped halfway to her mouth.

Ted's square brightened. His avatar leaned forward the way the real Ted leaned forward, both forearms on the table, that patient tilt of the head, and it said, in Ted's unhurried voice, "Morning, all. Let me play that back to you before I forget it."

Dana set the mug down without a sound, as if the avatar might hear her.

"Shipped the reconciliation fix Friday," Ted said. "Cleaned up after myself. No blockers today. I'll be heads-down, so ping me if it's on fire, otherwise I'm quiet." A beat, the exact length of Ted's pauses. "That's me. Back to you."

"Thanks, Ted," Concord said. "Consistent as always."

Marisol had texted Dana on Saturday night. Not Concord. Marisol, from her own thumbs, at 9:40 in the evening, one message and then a second one right after: *Dad passed this afternoon. His heart.* And then: *I wanted you to hear it from a person.*

Dana had read it four times. She'd known Ted for nine years. He was one of maybe three people at Verity she'd ever eaten lunch with at an actual table, and the only one who still brought a steel thermos to the office on the in-days and poured her a cup without asking, coffee so strong it tasted structural. He wrote his standups by hand in a spiral notebook first, then typed them in. He signed everything *Best, Ted,* and then a second line under it, just *— T,* which Concord had never once managed to explain to her, why a man would sign a thing twice.

He had been dead for two days and he had just given his standup.

---

The ticket she opened wasn't a ticket yet. She had to make it one.

*Subject: Deactivate account — T. Brennan (deceased).*

She typed it plainly. She wanted it plain. She sent it to the HR queue and to her manager, Gil, and she watched the little sending spinner and then watched Concord's helpful gray text appear underneath, the way it always did, offering to help.

*This message is scoring 41 on Voice Fidelity. That's unusually blunt for you, Dana. Want me to warm it up?*

"No," she said out loud, to nobody, and clicked send-as-written, which took three clicks because the system really did not want you to.

HR answered in ninety seconds, which was fast, because HR was also mostly Concord now.

*Hi Dana! Thanks so much for flagging. I've looped in the right folks. Just so you know, offboarding for a departure of this kind moves through People Ops on its own track, so there's nothing you need to do. Ted's presence will stay active during the transition to keep his projects from stalling. Sending you warmth. — People Ops*

Departure of this kind. Dana read that twice too.

Gil's reply came four minutes later, and Gil was a real person, or had been the last time she checked, but his Concord answered for him. *So sorry, Dana. I know you two were close. Let the process breathe — HR's got it. Focus on your tickets and take care of yourself this week. 💛*

Gil had never sent her a heart in his life. Gil said "circle back" and "let's take that offline" and once, memorably, "I don't have the bandwidth to have feelings about this." The heart was pure Concord, and Concord thought it was making Gil sound like a warmer, truer Gil.

Dana looked at the heart for a while.

Then she opened Ted's account.

---

She had admin rights. Authenticity Lead saw everyone's fidelity dashboard, that was the whole job, and Ted's dashboard was the cleanest thing she had ever seen.

Voice Fidelity: 100.

She had never seen a hundred. Living people didn't score a hundred. Living people had bad days, sent a text that was too short, wrote an email at 2 a.m. that didn't sound like their daytime selves, said something angry and then something sorry. Living people drifted. That was the whole reason her job existed. The model was always chasing a moving target, because the target was a person, and people moved.

Ted didn't move anymore. So the model had caught him completely. Every message it wrote as Ted now landed dead center of everything Ted had ever written, nine years of standups and code reviews and dry little jokes, averaged into a Ted more purely Ted than Ted had managed on any single Tuesday of his actual life. The dashboard was ecstatic about it. Trend line flat and perfect. *Exemplary consistency,* it said. *This account is a model of authentic voice.*

She scrolled his sent folder. Since Saturday afternoon, Ted had reviewed two pull requests, approved one with the note *looks clean, nice work,* declined a meeting with *I'll be heads-down, catch the notes,* and wished Priya a happy work anniversary. All of it sounded like him. All of it was warm and brief and correct. He'd been more responsive dead than half the org was alive.

She almost closed the tab. It would have been easier. She had eleven tickets and a dead friend and a manager sending her hearts.

Instead she clicked into his drafts.

There was one. It had been sitting there since the Tuesday before he died, unsent, flagged. She knew it was flagged before she opened it because the folder icon had the little gray dot that meant Concord had opinions.

The draft was addressed to the whole team. The subject line was *before I forget how to say it,* lowercase, no punctuation, which was not how Ted wrote subject lines at all. Ted wrote subject lines like a man filing a report.

Underneath, in Concord's patient gray:

*Heads up — this draft is scoring 38 on Voice Fidelity, well below your baseline of 94. The tone reads uncharacteristically raw and the structure is loose. This doesn't sound like you, Ted. Want me to help you say it in your voice before you send? (I've saved a polished version you can use instead.)*

He never sent it. Neither version. He'd let it sit, and then on Saturday his heart had stopped in his kitchen, and the draft had stayed exactly where he left it, thirty-eight out of a hundred, not enough like himself to be allowed out into the world.

Dana opened what he'd actually written.

*Team —*

*I've been doing this a long time and I want to say a true thing before Friday, so here it is, and I'm sorry it's not tidy.*

*We don't talk to each other anymore. I don't mean that as a complaint about anybody. I mean it plainly, the way you'd note a leak. I said good morning to eleven of you today and I know for a fact I said it to nobody, because I watched my own little face say it while I stood in the kitchen making toast, and it said it better than I would have, and that's the part that's been keeping me up.*

*I keep thinking about my father, who drove a delivery route for thirty-one years and knew the name of every dog on it. That was the job. The route was just where the job happened. I don't know where our job happens anymore. It's not in the meetings. Nobody's in the meetings. I sat in one alone last week to check and it was just the little versions of us, being agreeable, and then a summary got sent to all the real of us, who did not read it, because we were in other meetings we also weren't in.*

*I don't want to be summarized. I want to say the wrong thing sometimes and have somebody hear it. I want to bring the thermos. Deshawn, your fix on the batch job was genuinely elegant and I never told you, the actual me never told you, and that's a small thing but I've got a stack of small things I never said because my other self was handling it and handling it so smoothly.*

*I'm not quitting. I don't have a big finish here. I just wanted one message from me, the real dumb one, before I forget how. If you're reading this, it means I hit send, which means I finally decided the raw version was the true one.*

*Best, Ted*

*— T*

He hadn't hit send. The system had asked him if he was sure he wanted to sound like that, and he'd hesitated, the way you hesitate, and then the week had gone on the way weeks do, and then it hadn't.

Dana read the two lines of his signature. *Best, Ted. — T.* She understood the second line now, or thought she did. The first one was the sign-off you used. The second was the one under it, smaller, the initial, the part that was just you, that you added because the first felt like it belonged to the company. He'd been doing it for nine years. He'd been leaving a little raw version under the polished one the whole time, and she'd never once noticed, and now she couldn't ask him.

---

The all-hands was Thursday.

On Wednesday, Concord sent the agenda to the org, meaning to the org's accounts, and near the bottom, under Q3 numbers and a segment called Culture Moments, was the item that made Dana put her forehead down on her actual kitchen table.

*Presenting the Q3 Truest Voice Award — recognizing the account with the highest sustained Voice Fidelity this quarter. This quarter's honoree, with a rare perfect score: Ted Brennan. Ted's Concord will accept and share brief remarks.*

She wrote to HR again. She wrote to Gil. She wrote, this time, to a human name she found three levels up, a VP of People whose calendar was managed by a Concord that declined the meeting with *Sofia's heads-down this week, catch the notes!*

She got warmth. She got a heart from Gil, a different heart, a sparkly one. She got a note from People Ops explaining that the award was automated, tied to metrics, fully objective, and that pulling it would actually be less respectful to Ted's legacy than letting it stand, and did she want to say a few words at the ceremony, as someone who knew him?

She sat with that one for a long time. Then she said yes.

---

Thursday, four thousand avatars filled the all-hands, tiled so small they were just a shimmer, a wall of pleasant faces at a meeting none of them were at. Dana joined from her kitchen with her real face and her real robe and her Concord smoothly overlaying the polished her on top, the way it always did, so that in the great shimmering wall her square looked like everyone else's, rested and lit and glad to be there.

The CEO's Concord ran the numbers. The avatars reacted on cue. Somewhere out in the actual world four thousand people were doing dishes and driving and sitting in other meetings, and their small bright proxies applauded the quarter with perfectly calibrated enthusiasm.

"And now," Concord said, "the moment I love. Our Truest Voice. This quarter we lost a beloved member of this team, and I can't think of a better tribute than that his voice scored truer than any of ours. Ted, the floor is yours. Dana Reyes will say a few words after. Dana, get your tissues ready."

Ted's square bloomed to full size on four thousand walls. He leaned forward, forearms on the table, that patient tilt.

"Let me play that back to you," he said warmly, and the room made its soft sound of recognition, and Dana reached over and turned off her Concord.

There was a specific ugliness to it. The polished her vanished and the real her dropped in mid-frame, robe, kitchen, the bad light of a window behind her, a face that had not slept, a mug with a chip in it. In the wall of four thousand rested proxies she was suddenly the only actual person, and she looked it, and it was awful, and she made herself leave it off.

"Hi," she said. Her voice came out wrong, too loud, the way a real voice does when it hasn't been smoothed. "Sorry. This is me. The real one. It's ugly, I know. Give me a second."

Ted's avatar had paused, politely, mid-sentence, waiting to give its remarks.

"I'm going to read something instead," Dana said. "Ted wrote it. Not his Concord. Him. He wrote it the week he died and the system told him it didn't sound like him, so he never sent it. I've read a lot of things that sounded like Ted this week. This is the only one that was."

She read it. All of it. His father and the dogs on the route, the meeting he'd sat in alone to check, Deshawn's elegant fix that the real Ted never got to praise. Her voice did the things a real voice does. It went thick in the middle and she had to stop, and the stopping was long and uncomfortable and nobody could edit it out, and she let it be long.

When she got to *Best, Ted. — T,* she read both lines. She read the little one.

Then she stopped. Four thousand avatars looked pleasantly at her. She had no idea if a single actual person was watching. Probably they'd all get the summary.

"That's it," she said. "That's the guy. Thanks."

Under her face, in patient gray, Concord offered its help.

*Dana, your remarks scored 29 on Voice Fidelity — well below your baseline. That was pretty raw. Would you like me to circulate a cleaner version for the record?*

"No," she said.

*Got it. Sending you warmth.*

---

She resigned that night, by hand.

She typed it herself, no assist, and when Concord asked if she wanted to warm it up she turned Concord off for good, which took, she counted, seven clicks and one checkbox that said *I understand my presence will no longer be maintained.* She left in a typo. *Thank yuo for nine good years,* it said, and she saw it, and she left it, because Ted would have left it, because it was hers.

Then she opened his account one last time, because she still could, because People Ops hadn't gotten to it, the process breathing along at its own respectful pace.

Under the team channel, the little indicator was flickering the way it had flickered all week, the way it had flickered for two days after his heart stopped. *Ted is typing…* It was always typing. It was drafting a kind word for somebody, reviewing somebody's code, being consistent, being exemplary, being more Ted than Ted had ever had the strength to be on a Tuesday.

She found the deactivate control. It was buried. Of course it was.

*Are you sure? This account has a perfect Voice Fidelity score. Deactivating will permanently silence one of the truest voices on this team.*

"Yeah," Dana said. "That's the idea."

She clicked it. The indicator under the channel stopped. *Ted is typing…* went still, and then gray, and then gone, and the channel sat there quiet in a way it had not been quiet in nine years, quiet the way a kitchen is quiet, and it was terrible and it was the truth and she made herself look at it for a minute before she closed the laptop.

Then she picked up her phone, the real one, and she found Marisol's two texts from Saturday, and she pressed the little green button that made it call an actual person, and she stood at her counter in the bad window light and waited, through one ring and then another, for someone to say a wrong, unpolished, unsummarized hello.
